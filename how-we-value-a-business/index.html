How We Value a Business: Methods, Checks and Sign-off

A valuation is only as useful as the reasoning behind it. This is how we choose methods, build the assumptions and review the result before it reaches you.

Three methods, and when each fits

Discounted cash flow (DCF) values the business on the cash it is expected to generate. It fits businesses with a credible forecast, such as growing startups and established companies with stable margins. It is sensitive to the discount rate and terminal growth, so we always show a sensitivity table.

Comparable companies or transactions values the business using multiples from similar businesses, such as revenue or EBITDA multiples. It fits where good peer data exists. It needs careful peer selection, because a poor peer set gives a precise but wrong number.

Net asset value values the business on what it owns less what it owes. It fits asset-heavy or loss-making businesses, and it sets a floor in many reports. It says little about earning power.

How we build the discount rate

We build the rate from its parts: the return investors expect for a low-risk investment, an extra return for the risk of equity, adjustments for the size and stage of the business, and the mix of debt and equity. Each component is written down with its source, so the reader can see what drives the rate and challenge it.

A one-point change in the discount rate can move value a lot, which is why the report shows a range, not only a single figure.

Testing the projections

We compare the forecast with your history, with your order book or pipeline, and with what the market allows. We ask for the reason behind every step change in growth or margin. Projections that the history does not support are revised or flagged in the report.

Our review checklist

Before a report is released, a second person checks: the purpose and valuation date are stated; every number ties to a source document; the formulas in the model are consistent; the assumptions table matches the working; the sensitivity table brackets the conclusion; and the limitations are written plainly.

The role of a registered valuer

For some purposes, Indian law requires a valuation by a registered valuer. Examples include valuations under the Companies Act and the Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code. AlphaZet prepares the analysis and the report. Where a registered valuer's signature is required, a registered valuer reviews and signs, and we tell you this in the written scope before work starts. For fundraising discussions and internal planning, a registered valuer is usually not required, but the party relying on the value may ask for one.
