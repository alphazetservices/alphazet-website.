DSCR Explained: How Banks Judge Your Repayment Capacity

What the debt service coverage ratio is, the formula banks use, a worked example, typical ranges, why the lowest year matters and how to improve DSCR.

What DSCR measures

The debt service coverage ratio, or DSCR, answers one question: does the business earn enough cash to pay the loan instalments due in a year? A DSCR of 1.0 means the cash earned equals the payments due. Below 1.0, profits do not cover repayments. Above 1.0, there is a cushion.

Lenders look at DSCR in every term-loan proposal, because it shows repayment capacity more directly than profit alone. This guide explains the formula, gives a worked example and shows how to improve a weak ratio.

The formula banks use

The common bank formula is: DSCR = (net profit after tax + depreciation + interest on term loan) divided by (principal repayment + interest on term loan).

The numerator is the cash available for repayment. Depreciation is added back because it reduces profit but does not take cash out of the business, and interest is added back because it is part of what you must pay. The denominator is what you must pay in the year: the instalment of principal plus the interest. Banks sometimes add other non-cash items to the numerator, so confirm your bank's version.

A worked example

Suppose a fictional business shows these figures for a year, in rupees lakh: profit after tax 14.7, depreciation 22, interest 11 and principal repayment 18.

Cash available = 14.7 + 22 + 11 = 47.7. Payments due = 18 + 11 = 29. DSCR = 47.7 ÷ 29 = 1.64. The business covers its repayment 1.64 times. Try your own numbers in our free DSCR calculator, which works year by year and draws a chart.

What range do banks want?

Many lenders look for an average DSCR of roughly 1.25 to 1.5 or higher over the loan period. This is an indicative range and not a regulatory rule. Each bank sets its own norm, which can vary by industry, loan type and your track record.

Stable businesses with predictable cash flow may be accepted at a lower ratio. Cyclical or younger businesses may be asked for a higher one.

Average DSCR and the weakest year

Banks look at the average across the loan and also at the lowest year. A comfortable average can hide a year in which the cash does not cover the instalment, usually the first year after a moratorium or a year with heavy capital spending. Many credit officers want the minimum DSCR to stay above 1.0 and often above a stated floor.

That is why you should look at DSCR year by year, not only the average.

DSCR is not the only ratio

Banks look at DSCR alongside the interest coverage ratio, which compares operating profit with interest; the current ratio, which compares current assets with current liabilities; and the debt-to-net-worth ratio, which shows how much of the funding is borrowed. A strong DSCR with a weak current ratio can still raise questions about liquidity. A CMA report presents all of them together.

How to improve a weak DSCR

Whatever you change, reflect it consistently in your projections so the bank sees one coherent plan.

  • Lengthen the tenure: smaller instalments raise the ratio, although total interest rises.
  • Reduce the loan: a smaller loan or a higher promoter contribution lowers payments.
  • Ask for a moratorium: a repayment holiday during the build-up phase, if the bank agrees.
  • Improve profit credibly: price, cost or volume changes supported by evidence, not by assumption alone.
  • Phase the capital spending: spreading purchases over time reduces the funding needed upfront.

Reading DSCR in a projection

A final tip: when you present DSCR to a bank, show the calculation line by line for each year, with the inputs taken from your projected statements. A clear working invites trust, while a single unexplained figure invites questions about how it was reached.

In a typical term-loan projection, the first year after disbursement often shows the lowest DSCR, because the business is still ramping up while instalments have started. As profits grow and interest falls with the outstanding balance, DSCR improves. If your projection shows the opposite, with DSCR falling in later years, check whether capital spending or a step-up in costs is the cause and whether it is realistic.

Also compare DSCR with your cash balance. A business with a thin DSCR but a healthy cash reserve may be able to manage a weak year, while one with a good DSCR and no reserve can still be caught by a delayed payment. Banks look at both, so show your cash plan alongside the ratio.

Common errors

  • Using profit before tax instead of profit after tax.
  • Leaving out interest in the numerator or the denominator.
  • Treating working capital interest as part of the term loan.
  • Calculating DSCR only for the average and ignoring the weakest year.
  • Using a repayment schedule that does not match the sanction terms.

Questions people ask

Is a DSCR of 1.25 enough?

Many lenders look for around 1.25 to 1.5 or higher, but each bank sets its own norm. Confirm with your lender.

Why is depreciation added back?

It is a non-cash expense, so the cash available is higher than profit alone suggests.

Does DSCR apply to working capital loans?

It is mainly used for term loans. Working capital limits are assessed with MPBF and the current ratio.

Can DSCR be below 1 in some years?

A year below 1.0 means profits do not cover repayment that year. Banks may accept it only with an explanation and a plan.
