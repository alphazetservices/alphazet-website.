Financial Projections for a CC/OD or Term Loan: What Banks Check

How to prepare financial projections for a CC, OD or term loan: what banks check, how to set assumptions, the ratios that matter and the common errors.

Why projections decide the loan

A bank lends against what your business will earn, not only what it has earned. Projections are your argument that the loan will be repaid. If they are believable, the officer can sanction with confidence. If they are not, the request stalls, however good your past results were.

This guide explains what banks check in projections for a working capital limit (cash credit or overdraft) and for a term loan, and how to prepare numbers that survive questions.

Working capital and term loans are tested differently

For a working capital limit, the bank looks at your operating cycle: how long you hold stock, how long customers take to pay and how long suppliers give you. The limit is sized to the gap. Projections therefore need sales, costs and the holding periods behind them.

For a term loan, the bank looks at whether the project's profits repay the instalments. Projections must cover the whole tenure and show interest, principal and the cash left after repayment. The key ratio is the debt service coverage ratio, covered in our DSCR guide.

Start from history, not from the target

Banks compare projections with past results. Build your numbers from the last two or three years: sales growth, gross margin, expense ratios and working capital days. Then change only what you can explain. A projection that doubles sales in a year needs an order book, a capacity expansion or a contract to support it.

If you are a new business with no history, use evidence in its place: quotations, signed orders, market data and the experience of the promoters.

The assumptions banks examine first

  • Sales growth: is it consistent with capacity, market and history?
  • Gross margin: why does it change, and does the cost of inputs support it?
  • Operating expenses: are staff, rent and overheads realistic for the volume?
  • Working capital days: do inventory, receivable and creditor days match your actual cycle?
  • Capital expenditure and funding: how will each purchase be financed?
  • Interest and repayment: do they match the proposed loan terms?

Ratios the bank will calculate

From your projections the bank calculates the current ratio, the debt-to-net-worth ratio, the interest coverage ratio and the DSCR for every year. Each bank has its own norms. For debt service coverage, many lenders look for an average of roughly 1.25 to 1.5 or higher, and they also look at the weakest year. Calculate these yourself first, and if a year falls short, adjust the plan or the loan structure, or explain the reason.

Test a weaker case

Credit officers often ask what happens if sales fall by 10 or 20 per cent, or if customers pay more slowly. A good projection includes a downside case and shows that the business still meets its commitments. If it does not, consider a longer tenure, a lower loan amount or a smaller expansion.

A model built in Excel with an assumptions sheet lets you test these scenarios in minutes. See our sample financial model for the layout we use.

Common errors

  • Assumptions typed into formulas, so changes are hard to trace.
  • Interest calculated on the opening balance when the loan is repaid monthly.
  • Depreciation, tax or working capital left out of the cash flow.
  • A balance sheet that does not balance.
  • Growth in every year with no seasonal or cyclical dip.
  • Projections that do not match the figures given to the GST portal.

A simple way to build the first draft

Before you submit, ask a colleague or adviser to read the projections cold and explain them back to you. If they cannot follow the logic from sales to cash, a credit officer will struggle too, and a short note of assumptions on the first page will help.

Begin with a one-page summary for each year: sales, gross profit, operating profit, interest, tax and profit after tax. Then add the balance sheet items that move with sales: inventory, receivables and creditors, using your holding periods. Add capital spending and the loan schedule. Finally, check that cash from operations, less capital spending and repayments, leaves a positive cash balance each year.

Build this in a spreadsheet where each assumption sits in one cell and every number in the tables is a formula. Then you can change a single assumption, such as sales growth, and see the effect on profit, cash and DSCR immediately. When the draft balances, review each assumption against your history and write one line of reasoning for it. These lines become the notes the bank reads.

Questions people ask

How many years should projections cover?

For working capital, usually two to three years. For term loans, the full repayment period.

Should projections be conservative?

They should be realistic and supported. Overly optimistic numbers lose credibility, and overly cautious ones can reduce the limit offered.

Can I change projections after submitting?

You can submit a revised version if the bank asks or if facts change, with a note explaining what changed.

Do banks check projections against GST returns?

They often cross-check turnover, so keep your figures consistent.
