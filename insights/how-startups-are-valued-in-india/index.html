How Startups Are Valued in India: DCF, Comparables and Other Methods

How startups are valued in India: DCF, comparable companies, net asset value and other methods, when each fits and how to prepare for a valuation.

Why valuing a startup is hard

A mature business can be valued on its past profits. A startup often has little history, losses today and a story about the future. A valuation turns that story into a number by making assumptions explicit, testing them and showing the range of reasonable values.

No valuation is exact. Two competent valuers can reach different figures because they make different assumptions. What matters is that the assumptions are written down, reasonable for the stage and consistent with the evidence. This guide explains the main methods and when each is used in India.

Discounted cash flow (DCF)

DCF estimates the value of a business as the present value of the cash it will produce in future. You project free cash flow for several years, discount each year at a rate that reflects the risk and add a terminal value for the years beyond the forecast.

It works best when you have a credible forecast and reasonable visibility on costs and growth. It is very sensitive to the discount rate and the terminal growth rate, so a good report shows a sensitivity table. For very early startups with no revenue, a DCF depends so much on assumptions that it should be used with another method. Our free DCF valuation calculator lets you try the mechanics.

Comparable companies and transactions

This method values the business by comparing it with similar companies, using multiples such as revenue or EBITDA, or with similar deals. It is quick to explain and reflects what the market pays.

It works only where good peer data exists. In India, listed peers of a young startup are often much larger, and private deal data is limited, so the choice of peers needs care and a discount for size and liquidity may be needed. A multiple is an input, not an answer.

Net asset value and other methods

Net asset value values the business on what it owns less what it owes. It suits asset-heavy or loss-making companies and often sets a floor in a valuation, but it says little about earning power.

Other approaches are used for early-stage companies: the venture capital method works back from an exit value and the return an investor wants, and the scorecard or checklist methods compare the startup with typical funded companies at its stage. They are used in negotiations and as cross-checks.

Which method fits which stage

Use this as a general guide, not a rule.

StageUsual approachWatch out for
Idea or pre-revenueVenture capital or scorecard method, as a negotiation guideVery high uncertainty; weak evidence
Early revenueRevenue multiples with a DCF cross-checkShort history; growth that may not last
Growing with marginsDCF plus comparable companiesTerminal value dominating the result
Mature or asset-heavyDCF or net asset value, with comparablesStale asset values

What goes into a good valuation report

You can see this structure in our sample startup valuation report, which uses a fictional company.

  • A clear purpose and valuation date.
  • The information relied on and its source.
  • A review of the projections against history and the market.
  • A documented build-up of the discount rate.
  • Each method's working, a reconciliation and weights, with reasons.
  • A sensitivity table and a plain statement of limitations.

When a registered valuer is required

For some purposes the law requires a valuation by a registered valuer or a SEBI-registered merchant banker. Examples include certain valuations under the Companies Act and the Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code and some tax and foreign exchange purposes. For a private negotiation with investors, a registered valuer is usually not required, but the investor may ask for one.

We prepare the analysis and the report, and where a registered signature is required, a licensed professional reviews and signs. We tell you this in the written scope before work begins.

How to prepare

Have your financial statements, your projections with assumptions, your cap table and shareholder agreements and the purpose of the valuation ready. Be ready to explain every step change in growth or margin. The better your records, the shorter the process and the stronger the result. Questions people ask

What is the best method to value a startup?

There is no single best method. DCF suits companies with forecasts, comparables suit those with good peer data and early-stage methods guide negotiation. Reports often combine two or more.

How much is my startup worth?

That depends on your numbers, stage, market and the purpose. A valuation report gives a range with its assumptions.

Do investors accept a founder-prepared valuation?

They may use it as a starting point, but they usually run their own analysis. A documented report from a third party can speed up negotiation.

Is a valuation report valid forever?

No. It is as at a stated date. Material events or the passage of time can change the value, and some uses require a recent report.
