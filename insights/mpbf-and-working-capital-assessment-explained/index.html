MPBF and Working Capital Assessment Explained

How banks assess working capital: the turnover method, the second method of lending, MPBF, margin and the ratios that decide your cash credit limit.

What working capital assessment means

Working capital is the money a business needs to run day to day: to hold stock, to give credit to customers and to pay suppliers before it gets paid. When you ask a bank for a cash credit or overdraft limit, the bank first works out how much working capital your business needs and how much of that it is willing to finance.

That exercise is called working capital assessment. The result is a figure for the limit, and a margin that you must fund from your own long-term money. The methods below show how banks have traditionally arrived at it.

The cycle behind the number

Working capital need comes from the operating cycle: how long money is tied up from buying raw material to getting paid by the customer. A business that holds stock for 60 days and gives customers 45 days of credit, while its suppliers give it 30 days, needs to fund the difference.

That is why a trader with fast stock turnover needs less working capital than a manufacturer with a long production cycle for the same sales. It is also why delays in receipts increase your need quickly.

The turnover method

For smaller limits, many banks use a turnover-based approach. Under the commonly used version, the working capital requirement is taken as 25% of projected annual turnover. The bank finances up to 20% of turnover, and the borrower brings 5% of turnover as margin from its own funds.

For example, with projected turnover of ₹2 crore, the working capital requirement on this basis is ₹50 lakh, bank finance up to ₹40 lakh and borrower margin ₹10 lakh. The figures are for illustration only. The exact cut-off up to which a bank uses this method, and any variations, depend on its own policy.

The second method of lending and MPBF

For larger limits, banks have traditionally looked at the balance sheet. Under the second method of lending, the maximum permissible bank finance (MPBF) is 75% of projected current assets less other current liabilities, which means current liabilities other than the bank borrowing itself. The remaining 25% of current assets is the margin the borrower funds from long-term sources.

For example, if projected current assets are ₹100 and other current liabilities, such as creditors, are ₹30, MPBF is 75 less 30, which is 45. The borrower's margin is 25. Again, this is an illustration of the formula, not a promise of what a bank will sanction.

How the rules developed

These methods come from committees set up in earlier decades to bring discipline to bank lending. The Reserve Bank later withdrew its detailed prescriptions and allowed banks to set their own methods of assessment, and many banks continue to use the same logic or a variation of it in their own policies.

So the practical rule is to ask your branch which method it will use for a limit of your size. We cover the background and the numbers in our guide to the CMA report.

Ratios the bank checks

After the limit, the bank looks at the ratios in the CMA data to judge whether the business can carry it.

Benchmarks vary by bank, industry and loan, so treat any number you read online as a rough guide and use your bank's own criteria.

  • Current ratio: current assets divided by current liabilities. A higher ratio means more cushion. Banks commonly look for a figure above one, and often more.
  • Total outside liabilities to tangible net worth: how much of the business is financed by outsiders compared with the owners.
  • Debt service coverage ratio (DSCR) for term loans: how well operating cash flow covers the year's interest and repayment. See our guide to DSCR.
  • Inventory and receivable days, compared with industry norms and with your own past figures.

Questions people ask

What is MPBF in simple words?

It is the largest amount of bank finance the second method of lending allows for your working capital: 75% of current assets less your other current liabilities.

Is the turnover method the same as MPBF?

No. The turnover method looks at projected sales, while MPBF looks at the balance sheet. Banks may use either depending on the size of the limit and their policy.

Will my bank sanction exactly what the formula shows?

Not necessarily. The formula gives an eligible range. The bank also looks at your track record, security, industry and its own policy.

Why does my bank want a margin?

A margin means you fund part of your working capital yourself, which shows commitment and reduces the bank's risk.
