Pitch Deck for Indian Investors: The 12 Slides That Matter

The 12 slides an Indian investor expects in a pitch deck, what each should say, common mistakes and how to keep the numbers consistent.

What a pitch deck is for

A pitch deck is not meant to close an investment. It is meant to earn the next meeting. An investor may see dozens of decks in a week, so yours has to explain the business quickly, show that you understand your numbers and leave them wanting to know more.

Most decks that work have between 10 and 15 slides, one idea per slide and little text. You will present them live or send them by email, so each slide must make sense on its own. This guide lists the twelve slides that matter and what each should say.

Slides 1 to 3: who you are, the problem and your answer

Resist the urge to explain your technology at length. Investors first want to know whether the problem is real and large.

  • 1. Title: company name, a one-line description of what you do, your name and contact.
  • 2. Problem: the problem, who has it and how they handle it today. Use a real example, not a general statement.
  • 3. Solution: what you built and why it is better, in plain language. Add a screenshot or a simple picture if it helps.

Slides 4 to 6: product, market and business model

This is where careless numbers hurt most. If your market size is a figure from a report with no link to your customers, an experienced investor will notice.

  • 4. Product: how it works, in three or four steps, and what is live today.
  • 5. Market: who your customers are, how many there are and how you worked out the number. State your sources, and prefer a bottom-up estimate to a copied headline figure.
  • 6. Business model: how you make money, your price, your margins and what a customer is worth over time.

Slides 7 to 9: traction, go to market and competition

Be honest about what you have not yet proved. Investors forgive early-stage gaps more easily than overstatement.

  • 7. Traction: customers, revenue, growth rate, retention and anything that shows people want the product. Use real data only, and show the date.
  • 8. Go to market: how you reach customers and what it costs. Show what has worked so far.
  • 9. Competition: who else solves the problem, including the alternative of doing nothing, and why customers would choose you.

Slides 10 to 12: financials, team and the ask

You can browse a complete example in our sample pitch deck. It uses a fictional company, and its financial slide comes from the sample financial model.

  • 10. Financial plan: a three to five year summary of revenue, costs and cash, with the key assumptions. It must match your detailed model.
  • 11. Team: the people, what they have done before and the roles you still need to fill.
  • 12. The ask: how much you are raising, what you will spend it on and what milestones it will reach.

Keep the numbers consistent

The most common reason a good idea loses an investor's trust is inconsistent numbers: revenue on the traction slide that does not match the financial plan, or a funding ask that does not fit the cash flow. Build the model first, then draw the deck from it.

If you are valuing the company for the round, make sure the valuation, the model and the deck use the same assumptions. A short valuation walkthrough can help you explain how you reached your number.

Mistakes to avoid

  • Too many slides or too much text on a slide.
  • A market size copied from a headline report.
  • No evidence of customers or demand.
  • Projections that grow every year at the same steep rate.
  • Hiding risks instead of showing how you manage them.
  • Different numbers in the deck, the model and the data room.

Tailoring the deck to the investor

An angel investor reading a deck for a first meeting wants to see whether the problem is real and whether you can execute. A venture fund will look more closely at market size, the path to a large outcome and unit economics. A strategic investor will want to know how you fit with their business. Keep one core deck and adjust the emphasis, the order of slides and the opening lines for each audience.

Whoever you pitch to, keep a longer appendix ready: detailed financial assumptions, customer references, product screens and the cap table. Share it when asked, not before.

Before you send it

Read the deck aloud and time it. Ask someone outside your industry what the company does. Check every number against the model. Remove claims you cannot support. Then tailor the first slide or the covering email to each investor, showing you know what they fund. We design decks with a written scope and keep them consistent with your financial model.

Questions people ask

How many slides should a pitch deck have?

Usually 10 to 15. Fewer, clearer slides get read more often.

Should I include financial projections?

Yes, a summary of three to five years, with assumptions. Keep the detailed model ready to share.

Do I need a valuation in the deck?

Not always. Many founders state the amount they are raising and the use of funds, and discuss valuation in the meeting.

Can a good deck guarantee funding?

No. A good deck helps you get meetings. Investors decide on the business, the team and the market.
