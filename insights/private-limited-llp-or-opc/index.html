Private Limited, LLP or OPC: choosing a business structure

A plain comparison of Private Limited, LLP and One Person Company: ownership, funding, compliance, liability and which fits your plans.

Why the structure matters

The structure you choose decides who can own the business, whether you can take equity investment, how much compliance you carry every year and how banks and large customers see you. It is cheaper to choose well on day one than to convert later.

There is no single best structure. The right one depends on who the owners are, whether you plan to raise equity funding, whether you want to give employee stock options and how much annual compliance you are ready to handle. This guide compares the three most common structures for Indian startups and small businesses: the Private Limited company, the Limited Liability Partnership (LLP) and the One Person Company (OPC).

Private Limited company

A Private Limited company is a separate legal entity owned through shares. The owners (shareholders) are separate from the people who run it (directors), although in a small company the same people usually do both.

It needs at least two directors and at least two shareholders, and at least one director must be a resident in India. It can have up to 200 members, can issue different classes of shares and can grant ESOPs. For these reasons it is the usual structure for startups that expect to raise equity from angels or venture investors, and most investors are comfortable with it.

  • Owners: at least two shareholders and two directors.
  • Funding: can issue shares to investors and give ESOPs.
  • Compliance: annual accounts, statutory audit, annual returns with the Registrar of Companies, board meetings and director KYC.
  • Liability: limited to the unpaid amount on the shares held.

Limited Liability Partnership (LLP)

An LLP combines the flexibility of a partnership with limited liability for its partners. It is a separate legal entity, and the partners agree their roles and profit share in an LLP agreement.

It needs at least two partners, with at least two designated partners, one of whom must be a resident in India. Annual compliance is lighter than for a company: an annual return and a statement of accounts and solvency, with an audit only above the prescribed turnover or contribution limits. The main limit is that an LLP cannot issue shares, so it cannot give ESOPs, and most equity investors prefer a company. Professional firms and small businesses that do not plan to raise equity often choose an LLP.

One Person Company (OPC)

An OPC is a company with a single shareholder. It suits a solo founder who wants limited liability and a separate legal entity without bringing in a second owner.

An OPC must name a nominee who would take over if the owner cannot continue. It has company-style compliance, though some requirements, such as the number of board meetings, are relaxed compared with a Private Limited company. The rules on when an OPC must or may convert into another form have changed over the years, so check the current position with the Ministry of Corporate Affairs or a professional before you rely on an OPC for the long term. If you expect a co-founder or investors soon, you may prefer to start as a Private Limited company.

Which one should you choose?

Start with your funding plan. If you may raise equity from investors, or want to give ESOPs to your team, a Private Limited company is usually the safer choice because investors expect it. If you will not issue shares and want lighter compliance, an LLP can make sense. If you are a solo founder and want a company with limited liability, an OPC is an option, though many founders choose a Private Limited company with a second director to keep growth options open.

Then check the other factors: do you need credibility with large clients or banks, where Private Limited companies are most familiar; do foreign investors or foreign owners come in, where a Private Limited company is the usual route; and how much compliance effort you can manage. Cost and time

Costs depend on authorised capital, the state of registration, professional fees and the number of directors or partners. Government fees and stamp duty vary, so we show them separately in our written scope instead of quoting a single figure here. Incorporation usually takes a few working days to a few weeks after your documents are complete, depending on name approval and the authority's queries.

Whichever structure you choose, plan for the work after incorporation: opening a bank account, PAN and TAN, GST if needed, and your first annual filings. Missing them leads to penalties and late fees.

Common mistakes to avoid

  • Choosing an LLP and then finding that an investor wants shares.
  • Choosing an OPC and not planning for what happens when a co-founder joins.
  • Registering the entity before agreeing who owns what, which forces a costly change later.
  • Ignoring annual compliance until a notice arrives.
  • Using an address without the documents the registrar accepts.

Questions people ask

Can an LLP raise venture capital?

An LLP cannot issue shares, and most venture investors prefer a company, so startups seeking equity usually choose a Private Limited company.

Can I convert from one structure to another later?

Conversions are possible but take time and cost money. It is usually cheaper to choose the structure that fits your three-year plan at the start.

Which structure has the lowest compliance?

An LLP generally has lighter annual filings and audits only above prescribed limits.

Do I need two directors for a Private Limited company?

Yes. A Private Limited company needs at least two directors and two shareholders, and at least one director resident in India.
